A conversion audit checklist: 33 checks for a site that takes bookings or orders

Last updated: 13 November 2026

This checklist is built from what I go through when I audit a website for conversion: 33 checks in nine groups, written as questions you can answer by looking at your own site. None of them needs a tool or access to the code. Print the page and tick them off.

Before you start: four rules

  • Look at the screen, not at the code. What counts is what a visitor sees and can press.
  • Look twice. Pages load in stages and pop ups arrive late. Wait a few seconds and look again before writing anything down.
  • Write the page and the date next to everything you note. Sites change, and a note without a date cannot be checked later.
  • Do not put a number on what it costs you. A checklist shows what a visitor meets. Only your own figures, before and after a change, show what the change did.

The way to a booking or a purchase

  1. List every button and link that says book, buy, order or contact, on the home page and on each main page.
  2. Press each one. Does it land where its words say it will?
  3. Do they all lead to the same place, or do some still point to an older page?
  4. Does a button that says “Book” open a calendar or a list of times, or only a contact form?
  5. Can a new customer finish without first creating an account or signing in?
  6. If the booking or checkout page is on another company’s address, does it show your name and your logo?
  7. From a page that describes one service or product, is there a button to book or buy that one thing?

Prices

  1. For each main thing you sell: is there a price, or a starting price, anywhere on the site?
  2. How many presses does it take to reach it from the page that describes the thing?
  3. If a page announces an offer or a special, does it say what the offer costs?

Background: what two field experiments found about hiding prices.

What customers say

  1. Does the home page show anything a customer said: a quote, a rating, a review?
  2. If there is a reviews page, is it in the main menu, or only in the footer?
  3. Does the site point to the business’s reviews on Google or on another review site?

Background: what is and is not known about reviews.

Things that do not do what they say

  1. Does every link in the menu and in the footer open a page that exists?
  2. On a phone, does pressing the phone number start a call?
  3. Does pressing the email address open a new message to the right address?
  4. Does every icon that works as a link have words with it, or at least a text label?
  5. Do the buttons say where they lead, such as “See prices”, and not only “Learn more”?

Background: how common broken links and empty buttons are.

Leftovers

  1. Is there an offer, an event or a date on the site that has already passed?
  2. Is there text left from the template the site was built on, such as “Service Name” or “Add a description”?
  3. Read each main page once for spelling mistakes and for words in capitals that do not need them.

Background: what spelling mistakes do to trust.

The wording

  1. Does the first screen say what the business does, and where, in plain words?
  2. Do buttons that lead to different places carry different words?
  3. Is anything a customer has to do, such as booking or preparing for a visit, written in plain and easy to read type?

Background: three studies where only the words changed.

The first screen, on a phone

  1. Open the site on a phone in a private window. Does a pop up cover the main button?
  2. Can every line of text be read against what is behind it, including text placed over photographs?
  3. Is the main button visible without scrolling, and can it be pressed?

Background: what the “50 milliseconds” study measured.

What the site hands to search engines and AI assistants

  1. Does the page title say what the business sells and where it is?
  2. Is there a description, and does it say the same?
  3. Does the page code carry a business record, and do its name, address and opening hours match the visible page?
  4. Ask Google’s AI Mode for what the business sells, in its town, three times, at least a minute apart. Write down which businesses each answer names.

Background: what AI Mode did to clicks in a field experiment.

Advertising

  1. Look the business up in Google’s public ad library. Is it advertising?
  2. If it is, open the page an ad leads to. Does that page pass the checks above?

For that page alone there is a shorter list: the landing page audit.

What this checklist does not do

  • It does not tell you what your conversion rate should be. The figure usually quoted as the average is a 2014 median of ad accounts.
  • It does not rank the problems by how much they cost. No study I have read measures that for an ordinary business website.
  • It does not cover anything behind a login, or how fast the site loads. For speed, Google’s PageSpeed Insights measures your own page.

What to do with the answers

Sort what you found by how little it takes to fix. A link that points to the wrong page is a change of one line. Start there.

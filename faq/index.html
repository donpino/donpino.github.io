About the audit

What is a website conversion audit?

A review of a website that looks for the places where visitors who came to book, buy or get in touch give up, and says what to change. It starts where an SEO audit ends: someone has arrived on the site, so what stops them? There is a longer explanation, with a checklist and a real example, on this page.

Is a CRO audit the same thing?

Yes. CRO stands for conversion rate optimization. “CRO audit”, “conversion audit” and “website conversion audit” are three names for the same piece of work.

What do you check on a site?

The same things on every site: every button and link that says book, buy or contact, followed to where it ends. Whether a visitor can find a price. Whether the site shows what customers say. Links, buttons, phone numbers and email addresses that do not do what they say. Expired offers and template text that was never replaced. Wording that leaves a visitor unsure what is offered. What the site hands to search engines and AI assistants. And whether Google’s AI Mode names the business when asked for what it sells in its town. The full list is the conversion audit checklist.

What do I get at the end?

A PDF of five to seven pages, by email, within three business days. It opens with three things your site already gets right. Then two to four specific leaks, one to a page: what I found, why it matters, the words to change. It ends with the order I would make the changes in, cheapest fix first. You can read a full sample audit before deciding.

Can I get a refund?

Yes. If you read the audit and think it was not worth the price, reply within seven days of receiving it and I refund the full amount. You keep the audit and you do not have to give a reason. The details are in the terms.

What kinds of business do you audit?

Businesses whose website exists to get a booking, an enquiry or an order. The first sites I audited were medical spas, which is why the published sample is one. The checks are the same for any site with that job. If you are not sure yours fits, email me and I will tell you.

Do you need a login or access to my site?

No. I look only at what any visitor can see. I do not ask for logins, booking systems or customer data, and I do not create accounts or submit forms on your site.

Will the audit increase my sales or bookings?

I cannot promise that, and I would distrust anyone who does. I can promise that the problems in the audit are real and that I found them on your site, on the dates written in it. The service is new, so I have no client results to show you either.

Is the audit written by AI?

I use AI and I would rather say so. It helps on some of the work. Nothing in the audit rests on it alone: every finding is confirmed by a person against the page as it shows on the screen, not against the source code. If something cannot be verified, it does not go in.

How is this different from an automated website checker?

A tool reads the code of a page. Text can sit in the code and be invisible on the screen, such as grey letters on a dark photograph, or a button placed where nobody will reach it. Twice while preparing recent audits I found a promising problem in the code and then deleted it, because measuring the element showed a real visitor never sees it. An audit reports what a visitor actually meets.

Is there a call?

No. Everything happens in writing. The audit comes by email, you reply to it, and I answer myself.

About websites and conversion

What is a good conversion rate for a website?

There is no single number. The figure most often quoted, 2.35%, is the median of a few hundred Google Ads accounts in a 2014 company blog post, not an average of websites. I did not find a peer reviewed study that measures one across ordinary business websites. The useful comparison is your own site against itself, month on month. The sources are in this article.

Do visitors really leave if a page takes more than three seconds to load?

The “53% leave after 3 seconds” line comes from one month of analytics data on 3,700 mobile sites in 2016, and Google’s own sentence was more careful than the one that gets repeated. Slower pages do lose people: an experiment that slowed pages on purpose measured small and lasting drops. But the 53% is not a law. Details in this article.

Does a spelling mistake really cut online sales in half?

No study shows that. The claim comes from a single anecdote from 2011. What has been measured is trust: in a randomized experiment, text with five spelling mistakes was rated 8.9 points less trustworthy. None of the studies measured sales. Details in this article.

Is it true that 94% of first impressions are about design?

The number comes from a 2004 study of fifteen women reading health websites. 94% of their comments about rejecting a site were about its design. In the same study, 83% of the comments about the sites they trusted were about content. Design was the reason to leave, content the reason to stay. Details in this article.

Should I show prices on my website?

The evidence does not give one answer. In two field experiments with a retailer, moving the price one click later raised sales in the online store and lowered them in a deals email. So “always show prices” and “never show prices” are both stronger than the evidence. No study I found measured a price that is never shown. Details in this article.

Do online reviews change which business people choose?

Ratings on review sites do. In a study of restaurants on Yelp, an extra half star made 7 pm tables sell out 19 percentage points more often. What showing reviews on your own website does to bookings is a different question, and I could not find a study that measured it. Details in this article.

Does Google’s AI Mode send fewer visitors to websites?

In a randomized field experiment in 2026, people assigned to AI Mode had a click-through rate to outside websites 18.8 points lower. The experiment measured clicks, not sales, and it covered all kinds of searches, not only searches for a local business. Details in this article.

Does it matter that my Book button opens another company’s website?

Nobody has tested exactly that. The closest evidence is about a booking link in an email: 63% of the people shown a real NHS booking link were unsure or unlikely to use it, and what mattered was whether they could tell whose website it was. Details in this article.

How common are broken links and buttons with no text?

Very. In an automated scan of the top million home pages, 46.3% had at least one link with no text and 30.6% had at least one empty button. None of the sources measured what this costs in sales. Details in this article.

Does the wording on a website change what people decide?

In the studies I read, yes, for judgments and usability. One website tested 124% better on a usability score once its promotional copy was made objective, concise and scannable. None of those studies measured sales or bookings. Details in this article.

“Only 1% click a carousel” comes from a university home page. The rotating one on the same list got 9%

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Only 1% of users click on carousels.” I went looking for the study. It is two blog posts from 2013 by a web developer at the University of Notre Dame, who counted clicks on the carousels of five university websites. The 1% is the main university home page. And the one carousel that moved by itself, the kind most often blamed, had the highest click rate of the five.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “only 1% of users click on carousel sliders”. The AI summary said that “studies consistently show” about 1% of users click, and that of those, “84% to 89% only interact with the very first slide”. It listed auto-rotating slides among the reasons carousels fail.1 The results below it, from web agencies and a social media post, said the same, several naming Notre Dame. I opened that search once.

The source: one developer, five university sites

Erik Runyon, a developer at Notre Dame, published “Carousel Interaction Stats” on his own blog on 22 January 2013.2 He added tracking to the carousel on the university home page, nd.edu, and on four other Notre Dame sites, and counted clicks against visits to each home page, from mid October 2012 to January 2013. On nd.edu:

  • ~1%of home page visitors clicked a slide
  • 84%of those clicks went to the first slide; the other four got about 4% each

On the other three sites with carousels that did not move by themselves, between 1.7% and 2.3% of visitors clicked. Then the line that rarely travels with the figure: the only carousel that rotated automatically “averaged the highest number of clicks with 8.8% of homepage visitors clicking a feature.”

The update: six months, 3.7 million visits

In July 2013 he published the same counts for January to June 2013.3

  • 1.07%nd.edu, 3,755,297 home page visits, 89.1% of clicks on the first slide
  • 1.27%a departmental site, 37,688 visits
  • 1.51%an executive office site, 8,210 visits
  • 2.99%another departmental site, 26,900 visits
  • 9.41%a news site, 43,724 visits, the only carousel that played by itself

He writes that his team does not usually build auto-playing carousels “because we’re not fans”, but that the numbers for that site were “quite different”. On the news site the clicks were also spread more evenly: 54.6% on the first slide, then 17.9%, 10.8%, 8.8% and 8.0%.

His own conclusions in January were modest: what is in the first slot gets the most attention, the content has to be worth clicking, and three or four slides are better than more. The topic mattered too: on nd.edu the most clicked slides were about sports.

What these counts do not show

  • There is no comparison. Nobody measured how many people would have clicked a single fixed banner in the same place, so 1% cannot be called low or high.
  • A university home page is not a shop. Runyon himself describes nd.edu as “more general traffic” and the other sites as “specific audiences”. Its carousel was showing news, not offers.
  • The one rotating carousel was on a news site, where people come to read stories. It is one site, so the type of site and the rotation cannot be told apart.
  • A click is not a sale or a booking, and someone who read a slide without clicking is not counted at all.
  • Five sites, one university, 2012 and 2013, counted by one person and published on a personal blog. Not peer reviewed.

What I take from this

  • “1% click carousels” is not a law. It is the click rate of one university’s home page in 2013. On the same list the figure ranged from 1% to 9%.
  • The first slide is what people see. On every site in both posts it took the largest share of clicks, between 40% and 89%. Whatever matters most goes there, or goes on the page without a carousel.
  • Do not hide the one thing visitors need in slide three. If a booking link, an offer or opening hours sit in a later slide, most visitors in these counts would never have clicked them.
  • The rotating carousel result is a reason to measure, not a reason to switch. One news site is not evidence that rotation helps a business. If your site has a carousel, your own analytics can count clicks per slide the same way he did.

“A red button beat green by 21%”: one page, a few days, about 2,000 visits, and no conversion rates given

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Changing a button from green to red increased conversions by 21%.” I went looking for the test. It was run on one company’s home page in 2011, over a few days and a little more than 2,000 visits. The post never said what the two click rates were. And its author ended it by telling readers not to switch their buttons to red without testing first.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “red button increased conversions 21% hubspot test”. The AI summary said that changing the button from green to red “increased conversions by 21%”, and added, sensibly, that analysts put it down to contrast rather than colour.1 Below it, agencies, a LinkedIn post and HubSpot’s own blog repeated the 21%, some calling it conversions and some clicks. I opened that search once.

The source: one post, August 2011

“The Button Color A/B Test: Red Beats Green” was posted on HubSpot’s blog on 2 August 2011 by Josh Porter, about a test “we” ran on the home page of Performable, a company whose own software recorded the clicks.2 The original address now redirects to a different HubSpot article, so I read the copy saved by the Internet Archive on 9 September 2011.

What the post says was done:

  • The page was the home page of Performable’s own website.
  • One version had the green button Porter had designed; a copy had the same button in red. Nothing else changed.
  • The test ran “over a few days of traffic”, with “over 2,000 visits” in total.
  • What was counted was whether each visitor clicked the button.

The result, in the post’s words: “21% more people clicked on the red button than on the green button.”

What the post does not give

  • The click rates. 21% more is relative. If 10% clicked green, 12.1% clicked red. A reader asked exactly this in the comments: what were the two rates behind the 21%? In the copy I read, with 15 comments, there is no answer.
  • Any measure of chance. The post gives no confidence level and no test of significance. It does say that tests like this often need “thousands of trials”; this one had a little over 2,000 visits, split between two versions.
  • Anything after the click. Another reader asked whether the red button also brought more completed forms. The post counts clicks only.

So “conversions” in the retellings means clicks on a button, not sign-ups or sales.

What the author said about it

Porter expected a small difference and was surprised by the size. But his conclusion was cautious:

  • “As always, we cannot generalize these results to all situations.”
  • Performable’s colour scheme was green, black and grey, so red may simply have “contrasted nicely” with the green in the design.
  • “Therefore, do not go out and blindly switch your green buttons to red without testing first.”

Several comments under the post made the contrast point too: a button that differs from the rest of the page may draw more clicks whatever its colour. One reader wrote that they would use more red buttons from then on.

What this test does not show

  • That red works better than green. It shows that on one green page, in a few days in 2011, a red button got more clicks.
  • That sales or sign-ups went up. Only clicks were counted.
  • How big the effect really was. Without the two rates and the split of visits, the 21% cannot be checked.
  • It is a company blog post about its own product, not a study, and not peer reviewed.

What I take from this

  • The lesson is in the author’s own last paragraph. Test on your page, with your visitors. The 21% belongs to one page.
  • Contrast is the plausible part. A button that looks like the rest of the page is easy to miss. Check that yours stands out from what surrounds it, on a phone as well as a computer.
  • Ask for the two numbers. Any “X% more” from a test should come with the two rates and the number of visitors. Without them, a 21% gain could rest on a few dozen clicks.
  • The words on the button matter at least as much. I went through what is known about vague link and button text in the article on links and buttons.

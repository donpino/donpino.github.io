Expedia’s “$12 million form field” was not about short forms. It made card payments fail

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Expedia made $12 million a year by removing one form field.” I went looking for the source. It is a talk an Expedia executive gave at a software conference in 2010, reported by ZDNet. The field was not removed because the form was too long. It was confusing people into typing their bank’s address, and their card payments were being rejected.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “expedia removed one form field 12 million”. The AI summary told the story with a fair amount of detail: an optional “Company” field, customers typing their bank’s name and address, card checks failing, $12 million a year.1 Below it were LinkedIn posts, short videos and blogs, several of them turning it into a rule about keeping forms short. I opened that search once.

The source: a talk, reported in November 2010

ZDNet published “Expedia on how one extra data field can cost $12m” on 1 November 2010.2 It reports what Joe Megibow, Expedia’s vice president of global analytics and optimisation, said the week before at a conference run by SAS, a company that sells analytics software. The article says Expedia used SAS software to find the problem.

The story, as he told it:

  • Many customers clicked “Buy Now” after entering all their details, and yet the booking did not go through. In his words, “we weren’t taking the money.”
  • Analysts compared the failed transactions to find what they had in common.
  • Under the name, the form had an optional field labelled “Company”. Some customers put the name of their bank there.
  • Having done that, they entered the bank’s address instead of their own. The card check compares the address with the cardholder’s, so the payment failed.
  • Expedia deleted the field, and “overnight there was a step function [change], resulting in $12m of profit a year”.

He added that they had found “50 or 60 of these kinds of things” the same way.

What the retellings change

  • The cause. In the source, the problem was not that the form was long. One field was easy to misread, and the misreading carried into the next field and broke the payment. Many of the retellings I saw in the search results, which I did not open, use it as proof that every extra field costs money.
  • The field. The source says “Company”, under “Name”. One retelling I saw in the same search results calls it “Business name” and puts it just above the billing address.
  • The certainty. The figure is one executive’s statement in a talk. The article gives no calculation, no number of failed bookings, and no period over which it was measured.
  • A 2013 retelling. The story was retold in a 2013 book by Thomas H. Davenport and Jinho Kim, in an excerpt published by Harvard Business School.3 It adds that the team used “Web metrics data and server log files”, and says removing the field “immediately raised profits for Expedia by $12 million”, without “a year” and without a calculation.

What this story does not show

  • That shorter forms convert better in general. This was one misleading field.
  • How the $12 million was worked out. It may be sound, but it was not published.
  • It was told at a conference of the company whose software was credited with the finding. That does not make it false; it is a reason to read it as a case story rather than a measurement.

What I take from this

  • Read your form as a stranger would. The useful question is not “how many fields?” but “could anyone misread one of them?” A label like “Company” next to payment details invited a wrong answer.
  • Look at people who tried and failed, not only at people who left. Expedia’s customers had done everything right from their side. Failed payments and form errors are worth counting separately from visitors who simply went away.
  • Optional fields are not free. If a field is not needed, it can only cause trouble. That is a reason to remove it, but the reason is confusion, not length.
  • Fill in your own form. On a booking or contact form, try it once yourself on a phone, with an unusual answer: a long name, a different billing address. If it fails for you, it fails for customers.

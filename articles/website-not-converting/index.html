Why is my website not converting? What was measured, and what I find when I look

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

Search for this question and you get lists of seven or eight reasons, often with a number attached. I checked which of the usual reasons have a measurement behind them, and what was measured. Then I list what I actually find when I open a business website, because the honest answer to “why mine?” is that nobody can tell without looking.

Where I met the question

On 13 November 2026 I searched Google for “why is my website not converting”. The AI summary at the top gave four reasons: unclear messaging, too much friction, the wrong audience and a lack of trust. It also said that visitors decide within 3 to 7 seconds whether a site is for them.1 I have not traced that range to a study. What has been measured is below.

The usual reasons, and what was measured

  • “The page is slow.” An experiment that added delays of 100 to 400 milliseconds on purpose measured 0.2% to 0.6% fewer searches per user. The direction is clear and the size is small. The famous “53% leave after three seconds” is one month of analytics data from 2016. Sources.
  • “It does not look professional.” In a 2006 laboratory study, students judged the look of a page consistently after a glimpse. In a 2004 study of fifteen women, design was the reason given for leaving a site and content the reason for trusting it. Neither study measured a purchase. Sources: the first, the second.
  • “Mistakes and broken links.” In a randomized experiment, text with five spelling mistakes was rated 8.9 points less trustworthy. In a scan of the top million home pages, 46.3% had at least one link with no text. Nobody measured sales. Sources: typos, links.
  • “The wording is vague or full of hype.” One website scored 124% better on usability once its promotional copy was made objective, concise and scannable. That is usability, not sales. Sources.
  • “There are no prices.” Two field experiments moved the price one click later. In the online store, days with at least one sale went from 80% to 95%. In a deals email, buyers went from 2.61% to 2.31%. It depends on what people expect. Sources.
  • “There are no reviews.” On a review site, an extra half star made 7 pm restaurant tables sell out 19 percentage points more often. For reviews shown on a business’s own website I found no study. Sources.
  • “The booking link leads somewhere people do not trust.” Of the people shown a real NHS booking link, 63% were unsure or unlikely to use it. That was a link in an email, not a button on a website. Sources.

What these studies do not show

  • Most of them measured trust, appeal or usability. Only the price experiments and the restaurant study measured something people bought.
  • None of them was run on your website, with your visitors.
  • A list of reasons cannot say which one applies to you. Two sites can fail to convert for opposite reasons.

What I actually find when I look

These are seven things I found on real websites of small businesses that take bookings, each one seen on the live page. Four are in the published sample audit, with the dates they were checked.2

  • Every booking button opened a sign in screen, for an account a new customer does not have.
  • The price existed, but four steps away from the page that described the service. That page had no button to book.
  • The home page carried nothing a customer had said. The reviews page was one small link in the footer.
  • Asked three times for what the business sells, in its town, Google’s AI Mode never named it.
  • A booking page still listed the sample services of the template it was built on.
  • Four service buttons all carried the same two words.
  • A button that said “Book your appointment” led to a contact form with no calendar.

None of these needed a redesign. Each is a link, a sentence or a setting. They are also easy to miss when the site is yours, because you already know where everything is.

How to find out for your own site

  • First check that it is a conversion problem. Count how many people reached the page last month and how many did the one thing it exists for. If almost nobody arrives, the problem is upstream. When Google answers in AI Mode, far fewer people click through to websites.
  • Do not compare yourself with “the average”. The figure usually quoted is a 2014 median of ad accounts. Compare your site with itself, month on month.
  • Walk the way to a booking or a purchase on a phone, as someone who has never seen the site. The conversion audit checklist lists what to look at.
  • Change one thing, then count again. Only your own figures, before and after, show what a change did.

What I take from this

  • No study can tell you why your website is not converting. The studies show which things matter in general. Which one is in your visitors’ way is on your site.
  • The evidence is for direction, rarely for size. Errors lower trust and plain wording tests better. How much that is worth in sales, almost nobody has measured.
  • Start with what costs minutes. A button that points to the wrong page is fixed in one line.
  • This is what an audit is for. It lists what a visitor meets on the way to a booking or a purchase, with the page and the date each thing was seen. It does not predict a conversion rate.

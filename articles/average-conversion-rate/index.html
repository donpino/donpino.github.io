The “2.35% average conversion rate” is a 2014 median of ad accounts

November 2026 · Luca

Ask what a normal conversion rate is and you will be told 2.35%. I went looking for where that number comes from. It is real, but it is not an average, it is not about websites, and it was published in 2014.

Where I met the number

On 13 November 2026 I searched Google for “website conversion audit”. The AI summary at the top of the results said: “Industry data shows that the average website conversion rate is 2.35%, while top-performing sites reach 11% or higher.”1 I opened that search once, and AI summaries change from one search to the next, so you may see different words. The same figure is repeated on many marketing pages.

The source: one blog post from 2014

Both figures match a post by Larry Kim, the founder of WordStream, a company that sells tools and services for online advertising.2 The address of the post carries the date 17 March 2014. The page says it was last updated on 26 May 2026, and the figures in it are the 2014 ones.

The post describes what was measured. WordStream took the Google Ads accounts it could analyze and looked back three months. It removed accounts without working conversion tracking, accounts with fewer than 10 conversions a month, and accounts with fewer than 100 clicks a month. In the post’s words, that left “hundreds of accounts”. Then it plotted each account’s conversion rate.

  • 2.35%the median account: half converted above this, half below
  • 5.31%or more for the top quarter of accounts
  • 11.45%or more for the top tenth of accounts

The post also says that about a quarter of the accounts converted at less than 1%.

Three things that got lost in the retelling

  • It is a median, not an average. The sentence with the method reads “The median was 2.35%”. The opening of the same post calls it “the average landing page conversion rate”, and that is the wording that travelled.
  • It is about ad accounts, not websites. These are accounts for paid search advertising, so the people counted had clicked an ad. The post is explicit that the figures are for whole accounts: “this isn’t for individual landing pages”. It says nothing about visitors who arrive from a search result, a map, a link or a recommendation.
  • It is from 2014. The measurement covers three months before the post. Nothing in it describes how people buy or book today.

What this post does not show

  • It does not say what counted as a conversion. Each advertiser decides that: a sale for one, a form or a phone call for another. A rate built on sales and a rate built on form fills are not the same measure.
  • The accounts are those WordStream could analyze, with the smallest and the untracked ones removed. That is not a sample of all businesses.
  • The introduction speaks of “thousands” of accounts with $3 billion in yearly spend, the method paragraph of “hundreds”. The post does not give the exact number behind the 2.35%.
  • It is a company blog post, not a peer reviewed study, and the company sells the remedy.

A second benchmark says 6.6%

Unbounce, a company that sells a tool for building landing pages, publishes its own benchmark.3 Its 2024 report says it analyzed more than 41,000 landing pages, 464 million unique visitors and 57 million conversions from the previous year, and that “the median conversion rate across all industries is 6.6%”. Across the nine industries it shows, the median runs from 3.8% to 12.3%.

That is almost three times the WordStream figure, and both can be true. They count different things: one counts whole advertising accounts in 2014, the other counts single pages built for campaigns, where a click on a button can count as a conversion as well as a completed form.

What this report does not show

The pages belong to Unbounce customers, people using a tool made to make pages convert. It is the company’s own analysis, not peer reviewed. I read the public overview page; the full report is behind a form and I did not read its methodology section.

What I take from this

  • There is no single “average website conversion rate”. I did not find a peer reviewed study that measures one across ordinary business websites. The two figures above come from companies describing their own customers.
  • A benchmark means something only when you know what was counted. Whose visitors, arriving from where, and what counted as a conversion. Change any of the three and the number moves by a multiple.
  • The useful comparison is your own site against itself. How many people reached the page, and how many did the one thing the page exists for, last month and this month. That number has a known definition, because you chose it.
  • This is why I do not put a predicted conversion rate in an audit. An audit lists what a visitor meets on the way to a booking or a purchase, with the page where each thing was seen. Nobody has measured what your rate “should” be.

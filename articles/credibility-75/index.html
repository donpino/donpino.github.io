“75% of consumers judge a company’s credibility by its website design”: I could not find it in the Stanford research

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“75% of consumers judge a company’s credibility based on its website design, according to Stanford.” I went looking for the study. I did not find it. The Stanford page that the figure is linked to contains no percentages at all, and the 75% is in none of the lab’s papers I could open. The nearest number in that research is 46.1%, and it counts comments, not people.

How the number is quoted

On 15 November 2026 I searched Google for the sentence. The AI Overview at the top of the results stated it as a fact: “75% of consumers judge a company’s credibility based on its website design, according to landmark research from Stanford’s Web Credibility Research.”1 The results under it repeated the figure, as “consumers”, “users” or “people”. I opened that search once.

I then opened four of the pages that repeat it, to see what each one points to:6

  • One gives no link, only “research from Stanford”.
  • One links to another website’s list of statistics.
  • One, a 2018 post on Forbes, links to an infographic on another site. That address now answers “Page not found”.
  • One links to Stanford itself: a page called Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility.

So the only link that reaches Stanford reaches that one page.

The page the figure is linked to

The page is a list of ten guidelines, written by B.J. Fogg of the Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab in May 2002.2 I read all of it. It says the guidelines are “based on three years of research that included over 4,500 people”. Guideline 6 is “Design your site so it looks professional (or is appropriate for your purpose)”, with the comment “We find that people quickly evaluate a site by visual design alone.”

There is no 75% on the page. There is no percentage of any kind on the page.

Under the guidelines the page lists the research behind them: six works from the lab, plus unpublished research. I tried to open all six.

  • Four opened. I searched the full text of each for the figure. It is not there as a finding in any of them.
  • One is listed with “No online source yet”.
  • One I could not open: the address on the page did not answer, and the same file on Stanford’s current address answered “Not found”.

I also opened a seventh work, a larger study the lab published a few months after the guidelines. The 75% is not a finding there either. So I cannot say the figure was never written anywhere. I can say it is not on the page it is credited to, and not in any of the five studies I could read.

What the Stanford research did measure, part one: a questionnaire

The study closest in date to the guidelines is the Stanford-Makovsky Web Credibility Study 2002.3 I read the whole report.

  • 1,481 people answered an online questionnaire in the winter of 2002. 33.1% were from the United States and 57.1% from Finland.
  • They were given statements about a website, such as “The site has a typographical error”, and rated each from minus 3 (“Much less believable”) to plus 3 (“Much more believable”).
  • Nobody looked at a website. People answered from their general experience of the web.
  • The report says it has not been peer reviewed. Makovsky is a public relations agency and helped pay for the study.

The results are averages on that scale, not shares of people. “The site looks professionally designed” scored plus 1.54. Six statements scored higher:

  • +2.02“The site has proven useful to you before”
  • +1.83“The site provides a quick response to your customer service questions”
  • +1.67“The site lists the organization’s physical address”
  • +1.54“The site looks professionally designed”

The other three above it were a respected organization (1.97), a site updated since the last visit (1.65) and a contact phone number (1.56). An earlier version of the questionnaire, answered by 1,410 people in 1999, gave the design statement 1.55.5

The same report lists the things that lower credibility. “The site has a link that doesn’t work” scored minus 1.42 and “The site has a typographical error” minus 1.26. On this scale a broken link takes away nearly as much as a professional look adds.

Part two: 2,684 people looking at real sites

The larger study was run by the same lab with Consumer WebWatch, a project of the publisher of Consumer Reports, which commissioned it.4 It is the one study here in which people looked at live websites.

  • 2,684 people took part between 15 June and 15 August 2002. Each was given two live sites on the same subject, out of 100 sites in ten categories.
  • They said which of the two was more credible and could leave a comment on each. Comments were not required.
  • The researchers collected 2,440 comments and sorted them by what they mentioned. A comment could be counted under more than one heading.
  • People were recruited through charities, which received 5 dollars for each person who finished. The report says this “does not provide a representative sample of Web users”.

The headline result, in the report’s words: participants “mentioned something about the ‘design look’ of the site in 46.1% of the 2,440 comments”. It was the most common heading. The next two were how the information was organised (28.5%) and how focused it was (25.1%).

  • 54.6%of comments on finance sites mentioned design look, the highest category
  • 46.1%across all 100 sites
  • 38.1%on opinion or review sites, the lowest category

No category reaches 75%. The number 75 does appear in this report, in a different place: it explains how the sites were ranked, where a site that “won 75% of the time” against the other site gets a score of plus 0.50. That is a line about the scoring, not a finding about design.

What the 46.1% does not show

  • It is a share of comments, not of people. The report’s own summary rounds it to “nearly half of all consumers”, but the table counts comments, and not everyone left one.
  • A comment was counted whether it was positive or negative. The report notes: “Slick-looking Web sites frequently received negative comments.”
  • The authors say the setting pushed the number up: people took part to earn a donation for a charity, not because they needed the information, so they had little reason to look deeper than the surface.
  • They also say “design look” was the broadest heading, so many different things were counted under it.
  • They write: “we caution readers against becoming too attached to these particular values”.
  • It is from 2002. Nothing in it measures a sale, a booking or an enquiry.

What I take from this

  • I would not quote the 75%. I could not find where it comes from, and the page it is credited to does not contain it. If someone has the source, the contact address is on the about page and I will correct this article.
  • The research behind it says something smaller. In 2002, when people were asked to judge two sites, how a site looked was the thing they mentioned most often, in a little under half of their comments. That is worth knowing. It is not “75% of consumers”.
  • In the same research, small faults weigh about as much as looks. A broken link lowered the score nearly as much as a professional look raised it, and a typing error was not far behind. I wrote about links and buttons and about typos and trust separately.
  • Other design numbers have the same kind of story. See the 94% study, the 50 milliseconds study and the 38% survey.

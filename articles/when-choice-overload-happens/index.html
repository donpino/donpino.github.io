Too many options: when choice overload happens, and a car study where luxury brands gained from more variety

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

My last article on this topic, about the jam study, left a question open. The famous jam study found that fewer options sold more, and a 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average effect close to zero. So when, if ever, do too many options stop people from choosing? I went looking for the work that came after. A 2015 meta-analysis gives four conditions. And on the idea that luxury brands win by offering fewer options, the one large study I found points the other way.

Where the jam study article left off

In that article I read the jam study and the 2010 meta-analysis by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd. The jam result came from 31 buyers against 4. Across 50 experiments the average effect was "virtually zero", but the results varied a lot from one experiment to another. I wrote that a second meta-analysis, from 2015, had looked for the conditions behind that variation, and that I had not read it yet.

The 2015 meta-analysis: four conditions

Alexander Chernev, Ulf Böckenholt and Joseph Goodman pooled 99 observations from 53 studies in 21 peer-reviewed articles, with 7,202 people in total.1 I read the full paper.

They found four things that make it more likely that a bigger choice set backfires:

  • Choice set complexity. How the options relate to each other: whether one option is clearly better than the rest, how attractive the options are overall, whether they differ on the same features or on different ones, and whether they complement each other.
  • Decision task difficulty. How hard the task is made, whatever the options: time pressure, having to justify the choice, how many features describe each option, and how the information is presented.
  • Preference uncertainty. How unsure people are about what they want: how much they know about the category, and whether they already have a clear idea of the ideal option.
  • Decision goal. How much people want to keep the effort low: whether they are buying or just browsing, whether they are picking one item or picking which range to choose from, and how concretely they are thinking about the choice.

These definitions follow the paper's own. A 2024 review of 49 articles in Frontiers in Psychology describes the same four factors.3

  • 0.56effect size for the decision goal, the strongest of the four together with choice set complexity at 0.55; preference uncertainty was the weakest, at 0.32
  • 68%of the differences between studies that the model accounted for once the four conditions and the outcome measures were included

Without the four conditions, the average effect of more options was not significant, the same result as in 2010. With them in the model, the studies that compared a small and a large set without testing any condition showed a consistent overload effect, which the paper calls "a finding counter to the data reported by prior meta-analytic research". The authors also reran the studies of the 2010 meta-analysis through their own model and report results consistent with theirs. The 2010 meta-analysis had also looked for conditions and concluded that none could be identified, and the 2015 paper disagrees with it on the average effect too. What changed is the model: four conditions chosen from theory, tested together, where the 2010 one mixed conceptual and descriptive factors.

Two more results matter for a page. First, the size of the large set did not predict how strong the overload was: some studies found it with quite small sets, and others found little even with large sets. Second, when people chose which range to choose from, such as which shop, they preferred the bigger one. The authors read this as people underestimating the overload they will feel later, when they pick the actual item.

The publisher's page lists a correction to the 2015 paper, published online in July 2015. I have not read it.

A third look, in 2018

Blakeley McShane and Ulf Böckenholt reanalysed choice overload data with a method meant to handle studies that measure different things and are nested inside the same papers.2 Their summary says choice overload "varies substantially" with the outcome measured and with the four conditions above. I read only the summary, so I give no numbers from it.

Two things that reduced it in experiments

The 2024 review describes experiments where the same large set stopped causing trouble.3 I read these in the review, not in the original studies.

  • Grouping. In experiments by Mogilner, Rudnick and Iyengar in 2008, splitting a large set into groups reduced choice overload, and generic labels such as "A" and "B" worked as well as descriptive ones, according to the review.
  • Known brands. In experiments by Misuraca and colleagues, people chose between 6 or 24 mobile phones. With well-known brand names shown, there was no choice overload. With the same phones and no brand names, there was.

Do luxury brands win by offering fewer options?

This is the question that started this article: do high-end brands sell more because they keep the choice short? I looked for a study that tested it. I did not find one that measured sales. The closest I found measured how people see a brand, and it points the other way.

Koushyar Rajavi, Sina Golara and Sajad Modaresi, writing in Production and Operations Management in 2024, tracked the new cars on sale at more than 20,000 US dealerships from August 2020 to March 2021.4 They matched that with 273,991 answers from YouGov panelists who said they were likely to buy a car soon, about how strong each car brand seemed to them: value, quality, satisfaction, impression, recommendation and reputation, averaged into one score. Variety meant how spread out the dealer's stock was across different model and trim combinations.

  • −0.032the average link between variety and how strong a brand seemed, not statistically significant
  • +0.162the extra effect of variety for luxury brands, statistically significant: more variety, stronger image

For luxury brands, more variety went with a stronger brand image. Wealthier respondents also valued variety more. The authors' reading is that luxury buyers want to feel unique, which is easier with more to choose from. For ordinary brands, they write, the better course for how the brand is seen is "focusing on fewer products and keeping it simple".

The same study found the opposite for stock levels. Plenty of cars on the lot helped ordinary brands more than luxury ones, which the authors link to the exclusivity luxury buyers want. So in this data, luxury brands gained more from variety and less from plenty of stock, which is not the same as gaining from fewer options.

What these studies do not show

  • None of them tested a website. The meta-analyses pool experiments on many kinds of choices, and the car study looked at dealer stock.
  • The car study measured how people rated brands, not what they bought. It is observational, in one industry, during the shortages of the COVID period. The authors call it an observational study and ask for field experiments to test it.
  • The 2015 paper tested each condition on its own. The authors write that very few studies have looked at two conditions together.
  • I read the 2018 reanalysis only in summary, the correction to the 2015 paper not at all, and the grouping and brand experiments only as described in a review.
  • No study here says how many options your page should have.

What I take from this

  • "Fewer options always sell more" is not what the research says. The better question is whether your visitors meet the four conditions: hard to compare options, a demanding page, people unsure what they want, and people ready to pick one now rather than browsing.
  • On a website, most of those are things you can change without removing anything. Show the same few facts for every option, so they can be compared. Make one option the clear place to start, since sets with a clearly better option caused less overload. Say who each option is for. Group a long list. This is my reading of the conditions, not a result tested on websites.
  • A known name may do the work of a short list. In the phone experiments, brand names alone removed the overload. For a small business that may mean naming options in words customers already use.
  • Be careful with "luxury means fewer choices". The one large study I found, on cars, saw the opposite for variety. What helped luxury brands less was plenty of stock, not a long menu.
  • If it matters for your page, test it. Three meta-analyses agree that the effect depends on the situation.

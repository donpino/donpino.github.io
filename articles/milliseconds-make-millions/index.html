“0.1 seconds faster, 8.4% more sales”: a Google-commissioned correlation. For form sites, conversions went down

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“A 0.1 second faster mobile site lifts retail conversions by 8.4%.” I went looking for the study and read the report. The figure is real, and it is a correlation found in four weeks of data from 15 retailers in 2019. The report was commissioned by Google. And for the kind of site most small service businesses have, one that asks visitors to fill in a form, the same report found conversion rates went slightly down.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “0.1 second faster site speed 8.4% conversions deloitte”. The AI summary said a 0.1 second improvement “increases retail conversion rates by 8.4%”, and listed other sectors, including “Lead Generation: Form progression increased by 21.6%”.1 The results below it repeated 8.4% on LinkedIn, Instagram and agency blogs. I opened that search once.

The source: a report commissioned by Google

The report is called Milliseconds Make Millions, 51 pages, published on Google’s Think with Google site; the file was created in March 2020.2 Its disclaimer sets out who did what:

  • It was “commissioned by Google and prepared by Deloitte Ireland LLP”.
  • The data was collected and analysed by a company called Fifty-Five, “recruited directly by Google”.
  • “Deloitte LLP did not audit nor validate this data.”

So the figure people attribute to Deloitte is Fifty-Five’s analysis, written up by Deloitte, for Google.

What was measured

  • Over 70 brands were approached and 37 qualified: 15 retail, 6 travel, 10 luxury and 6 lead generation, mostly in Europe and the United States.
  • Data came from four weeks, 28 October to 24 November 2019, about 30 million mobile sessions.
  • Speed was measured with Lighthouse, Google’s testing tool, which the report says uses “simulated lab data”. It was combined with each brand’s hourly analytics.
  • Nobody slowed or sped up any site. “Fluctuations in speed all occurred naturally”, and a regression model related them to what visitors did.
  • The “0.1 seconds” is an improvement of 0.1 seconds in each of four speed measures at once.

For retail, based on 20.5 million sessions: transactions up 8.4%, average order value up 9.2%, pages per session up 5.2%. For travel, conversions up 10.1%.

The result that rarely travels

Lead generation sites are, in the report’s words, sites whose purpose is to “get users to fill in a form”. The report’s own examples for this sector are finance and insurance companies. Based on 505,000 sessions:

  • +21.6%progression from the first step of the form to the form submission page
  • −8.3%bounce rate on informational pages on mobile
  • −1.9%conversion rate on mobile

The key findings page for this sector says it plainly: “Conversion rates on both mobile and desktop decreased when site speed was seen to improve.” The report does not explain the drop. The 21.6% is the figure that gets quoted for lead generation; it is one step inside the form, not the conversion rate.

What this report does not show

  • That speed caused the changes. It is a correlation in natural variation over four weeks. The report itself calls it a correlation.
  • What real visitors waited. Speed came from a lab tool, not from visitors’ own phones and connections.
  • Anything about small businesses. These were 37 large brands; the report warns its results “may not fully reflect the Internet as a whole”.
  • Anything about desktop. The authors say desktop data gave “a lot of contradicting parameters”.
  • It was commissioned by Google, which also makes the speed tool used to measure the sites. The data was not audited by the firm whose name is on it.

What I take from this

  • Quote it as a correlation, and say who commissioned it. “A Google-commissioned report found that, for 15 retailers over four weeks in 2019, 0.1 seconds faster went with 8.4% more transactions” is accurate. “Deloitte proved” is not.
  • If your site collects enquiries, the retail figure is not yours. In the same report, the sector closest to a service business saw its mobile conversion rate dip.
  • Speed still matters, in the way the better evidence says. The controlled experiments I found, at Google and Bing, show small losses from added delay. I went through them in the article on Amazon’s 100 milliseconds and the one on the three second rule.
  • For a form, check that it works before checking how fast it is. A form that fails on a phone loses every enquiry; a tenth of a second does not.

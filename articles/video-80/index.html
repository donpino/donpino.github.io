“Video on a landing page lifts conversions 80%” comes from a video company’s own marketing: “in some cases”

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Adding a video to a landing page increases conversions by 80%.” I went looking for the source. It leads to EyeView, a company that sold video for websites. Its white paper does not say video lifts conversions by 80%. It says that “in some cases the boost in conversion was over 80%”. Its first worked example was almost 32%.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “video on landing page increases conversions by 80%”. The AI summary said a video “can increase conversion rates by up to 80% or more, according to industry research by EyeView and I-view”.1 The results below it, mostly from video production companies and marketing blogs, gave the 80% to EyeView, to Unbounce, or to no one. I opened that search once. I looked only for the EyeView source.

The source: a white paper from a video company

The paper is called Making Video Accountable, by EyeView, which it describes as “the leading provider of video solutions for performance advertisers”, with offices in Boston and Tel Aviv.2 It carries no date. The text refers to 2010, and the copy I read is hosted on a marketing blog in a folder dated September 2012.

Most of the paper argues that companies put video on their sites without measuring whether it helps, and that they should test it like any other part of the page. That is a fair point. Then the paper describes the company’s own work:

  • EyeView says it has run “hundreds of tests on live traffic for over one hundred customers”, usually starting with the same page with and without a video.
  • “In almost every instance” the page with the video converted better.
  • “In some cases the boost in conversion was over 80%”.

That sentence is one source of the figure. A brochure by the same company has another: a manager at TutorVista, one of its clients, is quoted saying “the very first test they ran boosted conversion by over 80 percent”.3 The white paper does not say how many cases, what the typical result was, or how conversion was defined.

The two examples in the paper

  • ~32%eToro, a currency trading platform: the same landing page with and without an embedded video, half of new visitors to each
  • 86%TutorVista, an online tutoring service, “after several rounds of testing” launch styles, autoplay with and without sound, and animations after the video

Neither example in the white paper gives the number of visitors, the two conversion rates, the dates, or what counted as a conversion. The paper says both results were statistically significant, without the figures. The two EyeView texts tell the 86% in two ways. The white paper says it came “after several rounds of testing”. The brochure says it came from testing the original page against the same page “with the video autoplaying once for first-time visitors”, and gives the goal: a click on the Subscribe button. The brochure gives no visitors or rates either.

What this paper does not show

  • That video lifts conversions by 80% in general. The paper says “in some cases”, and its own first example is 32%.
  • Anything that can be checked. No sample sizes, rates or dates are given for any test.
  • The tests that did not work. The paper says “almost every instance” improved, without saying how many did not.
  • It was written by a company selling the product being measured, to promote it. It is not peer reviewed.

What I take from this

  • Do not quote 80% as an average. The source never gave one. “A video vendor reported lifts above 80% in some of its clients’ tests” is as far as it goes.
  • The paper’s own advice is the useful part. Test a video against the same page without it. A video added because everyone has one is, in the paper’s words, an “unregulated extra”.
  • A video should not carry what the page needs to say. Price, place, what is included and the button to book belong in text a visitor can read in seconds. A video can add to that; it cannot replace it. The paper itself says “most of your audience will not stick around for more than [a] minute of video”. The rest is my reading, not something the paper measured.
  • Check what it costs in speed. A heavy video at the top of a page can slow the first screen on a phone. I covered what is known about speed in the article on “Milliseconds Make Millions”.

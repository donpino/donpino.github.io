“94% of first impressions are about design” comes from 15 women in 2004

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Web design influences 94% of first impressions.” You will find that line on many lists of website statistics. I went looking for the study. It exists, it is peer reviewed, and it says something narrower and more interesting than the headline.

How the number is quoted

One example, opened on 13 November 2026: a list of web design statistics published by Hostinger, a company that sells web hosting, carries the heading “Web design influences 94% of first impressions”.1 The paragraph under that heading gives no source for the figure.

The study: fifteen women, four weeks, 2004

The 94% appears in a paper presented at CHI 2004, a peer reviewed conference on how people use computers, by researchers at Northumbria University and Sheffield University.2

Fifteen women aged 41 to 60, at various stages of the menopause, came to four weekly two hour sessions in an internet café in Newcastle upon Tyne. They searched the web for information and advice about the menopause, spoke their thoughts aloud while they did it, and then talked the sites over in a group. In two sessions they searched freely; in the other two they were sent to specific sites. Everything they said was transcribed and sorted by theme.

The researchers then counted the comments. Among the comments about rejecting or mistrusting a site:

  • 94%were about design: busy layout, pop up adverts, small print, too much text, dull colour, a corporate look, a poor name
  • 6%were about content that was irrelevant or inappropriate

That is the source of the statistic. It is a share of comments, made by fifteen people, about why they left a site quickly.

The number that rarely gets quoted

The same paper has a second table, for the sites the women chose to keep reading and said they trusted. There the proportions turn over:

  • 83%of comments about selected, trusted sites were about content: informative, unbiased, clear language, a wide range of topics
  • 17%were about design: clear layout, good navigation, interactive features

The authors read the two tables together as a model in stages. Design decided which sites were thrown out in the first moments. Content decided which of the remaining sites were trusted. In their words, design appeal predicted rejection, and the credibility and personal relevance of the information predicted selection.

What this study does not show

  • It is not 94% of people, and not 94% of first impressions. It is 94% of the comments about rejection. The paper gives the percentages as an indication of relative importance.
  • Fifteen participants, all women, all looking for health advice on one topic. It is a careful observation, not a sample of the public.
  • The sites were health websites of 2004. Some of what put people off, like pop up adverts and slow introductions, belongs to that period.
  • It recorded what people said while searching, not whether they later acted on any advice. The authors raise the possibility that people answer differently in a group, and say they found no sign of it.
  • Nothing in it concerns buying, booking or the website of a business.

What I take from this

  • The headline keeps half of the finding. In this study design was the reason to leave, and content was the reason to stay and trust. A list that quotes the 94% without the 83% tells you to polish the surface and stops there.
  • The things that drove people away were ordinary. A busy page, too much text, small print, adverts in the way, not knowing where to click. None of it needs a redesign to fix.
  • It is one small study. I would use it as a description of how fifteen careful readers behaved, not as a law with a percentage attached.
  • Both stages go into an audit. What a visitor meets in the first screen, and whether the page then answers the question they came with, each noted on the page where it was seen.

The 2000 jam study: 31 buyers against 4, on two Saturdays. Fifty experiments later, the average effect was zero

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Offer 6 options instead of 24 and ten times as many people buy.” I went looking for the study. It is real and it is carefully described, and the famous percentages come from 31 buyers against 4, on two Saturdays in one upscale supermarket. When the same kind of test was repeated, the effect mostly did not come back. Across 50 experiments, its average was close to zero.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “jam study too many choices website conversion”. The AI summary began: the jam study “proves that offering too many choices destroys conversion rates”, with “10x higher conversion rate” for 6 jams against 24.1 Below it, marketing blogs and short videos said the same. I opened that search once.

The study: two Saturdays in Menlo Park

Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper published the study in 2000 in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.2 The jam experiment is Study 1 of three. What they did:

  • A tasting table in Draeger’s, an upscale supermarket in Menlo Park, California, known for its range: the paper says it stocks over 300 kinds of jam.
  • On two Saturdays, five hours each, the table showed either 24 flavours or 6, switching every hour.
  • Everyone who stopped got a coupon for $1 off a jar of that brand, valid for a week. Buying meant walking to the jam shelf, where all the flavours were.
  • Purchases were counted from the coupons handed in.
  • 60%of the 242 shoppers who passed the 24 jam table stopped (145 people)
  • 40%of the 260 who passed the 6 jam table stopped (104 people)
  • 3%of those who stopped at 24 jams bought: 4 people
  • 30%of those who stopped at 6 jams bought: 31 people

So the large display attracted more people and the small one sold more jam. The difference is statistically clear. On average people tasted one or two jams whichever table they were at.

The authors list limits themselves. People at the 6 jam table may have thought those six were special. The two displays may have drawn different shoppers: the big one curious passers-by, the small one people who really wanted jam. That is why they ran two further studies, on essay topics and chocolates, which, according to the paper’s summary, also found that fewer options worked better.

When others tried again

In 2010 Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd published a meta-analysis in the Journal of Consumer Research: a study that pools all the comparable experiments they could find.3 Two things in it matter here.

First, a direct repeat. Scheibehenne had tried to reproduce the jam study in an upscale supermarket in Germany and “did not find any negative effects of choice overload”. An attempt with exotic chocolates in the lab, and one with jelly beans, found nothing either. The authors note small differences from the originals, such as country and procedure.

Second, the pooled result:

  • 50published and unpublished experiments, 63 conditions, 5,036 people
  • 0.02average effect size, which the authors call “virtually zero”

Not every study found nothing. The results varied a lot from one experiment to another, some finding fewer choices better and some finding more choices better, and the authors could not identify conditions under which too much choice reliably hurts. They also found signs that unpublished and more recent studies were less likely to find the effect.

A second meta-analysis, by Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, appeared in 2015 and looked for those conditions. I read it later, in a follow-up article.

What the jam study does not show

  • Nothing about websites. It was a tasting table in a shop, and the 3% and 30% are of people who had already stopped.
  • It is not “ten times the conversion rate” of a page. Counted against everyone who walked past, it was 4 buyers in 242 and 31 in 260.
  • One store, two days, one brand, in a paper from 2000. The direct repeat in another country found no effect.
  • It cannot tell you how many options your page should have.

What I take from this

  • “The jam study proves” is too strong. It is a well run field experiment with small numbers, and later work found the effect unreliable. Quote the 31 and the 4, not “10x”.
  • Look at how hard the options are to compare, not only how many there are. The 2010 authors suggest that ease of comparison may be one of the conditions that matter. On a website that means things like prices shown, names a visitor understands, and a plain line on who each option is for. This is a suggestion from the paper, not a tested result.
  • Cutting options has a cost the jam study did not measure. A customer who wanted the option you removed has nothing left to choose. Grouping options or marking a starting point keeps them.
  • If you want to know, test it on your own page. Fifty experiments disagreeing means the answer depends on the situation.

“38% of people stop engaging with an unattractive website” was not a question about websites

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“38% of people will stop engaging with a website if the content or layout is unattractive.” I went looking for the survey behind that line. It exists and the 38% is in it. But the question was not about websites, people were asked what they would do and not watched doing it, and in the same answer most of them said they would carry on.

How the number is quoted

On 15 November 2026 I searched Google for the sentence. Nearly every result on the first page repeated it, on company blogs and in social media posts. One of them, as shown on the results page: “38% of people will stop engaging with a website if the content/layout is unattractive.”1 Some name Adobe as the source. I opened that search once and did not open each result.

The source: an Adobe survey from 2015

The figure is in a report called The State of Content: Expectations on the Rise, published by Adobe in October 2015.2 Adobe sells design software. The methodology page of the report says:

  • 2,008 consumers in the United States, aged 18 or older, who use at least one digital device.
  • The answers were collected from 12 to 16 September 2015.
  • The survey was split into two questionnaires of 20 minutes, answered by 1,002 and 1,006 people. The question behind the 38% was put to all 2,008.
  • The stated margin of error for the whole sample is plus or minus 2.19.

The report does not say how the people were recruited.

The address where Adobe published the report now answers “Page not found”. I read it in full, 24 pages, in a copy kept on another website.

What the question asked

The question, word for word: “When you are viewing content on any device, which of the following would cause you to switch to another device to view the same content or stop viewing it altogether?”

So it is about content on any device. The report’s own list of devices and services includes television, streaming services, e-readers, messaging and podcasts next to websites. The word “website” is not in the question.

One of the situations listed was “The content is unattractive in its layout or imagery”. People answered it like this:

  • 38%stop engaging
  • 35%switch devices, to view the same content
  • 28%unchanged

The quoted sentence keeps the first box. Put the other two together and about six people in ten said they would keep going, on another device or on the same one. (The three figures add up to 101, as printed in the chart.)

The rest of the same chart

The chart has nine situations. Unattractive layout is not the one that loses the most people. These are the shares who said they would stop:

  • 39%“Images won’t load”
  • 39%“It takes too long to load”
  • 38%“Content was too long”

The difference shows in the last column, the people for whom nothing changes. With an unattractive layout, 28% said they would carry on as before. When images do not load it was 15%, when loading takes too long 17%, and when “links and buttons don’t work” 16%. In this survey, things that are broken or slow moved more people than things that are ugly.

What this survey does not show

  • People said what they would do. Nobody was shown a page and observed.
  • “Unattractive” was not defined. Each person answered with their own idea of it.
  • It is about content in general, on any device, in 2015. It is not about the website of a business.
  • “Stop engaging” is not “leave and buy elsewhere”. The survey has no measure of sales, bookings or enquiries.
  • It was run and published by a company that sells design software. It is not peer reviewed.

What I take from this

  • The sentence changes the subject. “Content on any device” became “a website”, and a three way answer became one number.
  • On the same chart, broken beats ugly. Images that do not load, slow loading and links that do not work left fewer people unmoved than an unattractive layout did. Those are also cheaper to fix than a redesign. I wrote about links and buttons and about loading time separately.
  • It is still what people say, not what they do. For first impressions there are studies that showed people real pages: the 50 milliseconds study and the 94% study. They have their own limits.
  • In an audit I note what can be seen. A button that goes nowhere or an image that does not load is a fact on a page. “Unattractive” is an opinion, and I would not put a percentage on it.

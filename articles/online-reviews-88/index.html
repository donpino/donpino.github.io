“88% trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations” is from 2014. The same survey now says 49%

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“88% of consumers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations.” I went looking for where that comes from. It is a real survey result, from 2014. It adds together three answers that each came with a condition. And the company that ran the survey has published a new edition this year: the figure in it is 49%.

Where I met the number

On 15 November 2026 I searched Google for the sentence itself. The AI summary at the top of the results said: “An estimated 88% of consumers trust online reviews just as much as a personal recommendation from a friend or family member.”1 It gave no year. I opened that search once, and AI summaries change from one search to the next, so you may see different words.

The figure is not only on marketing pages. A 2020 paper in the International Journal of Production Economics, about online reviews and customer returns, says in its introduction that 88% of consumers who read reviews “trust the online reviews as much as personal recommendations”.2 The reference it gives for that sentence is a 2015 infographic on a marketing website. I did not open the infographic.

The source: a company survey from 2014

The number is in the Local Consumer Review Survey 2014, published by BrightLocal, a company that sells software for local search marketing.3 The page says how it was done:

  • BrightLocal contacted its own consumer panel of “just under 5,000” people and received 2,104 answers.
  • 90% of the people who answered were in the United States and 10% in Canada.
  • The survey ran for six weeks in May and June 2014.
  • It was about choosing local businesses. In the survey’s own words, it is “not wider product reviews”.

The page does not say how people joined the panel, or whether the panel was built to match the population.

What “88%” adds up

Question 8 asked: “Do you trust online customer reviews as much as personal recommendations?” The chart on the page shows four possible answers. Three of them begin with “yes”, and each one carries a condition:

  • 32%“Yes, if there are multiple customer reviews to read”
  • 30%“Yes, if I believe the reviews are authentic”
  • 26%“Yes, for some types of business, no for others”

32, 30 and 26 make 88. The fourth answer was “No”, at 13%. The chart shows no answer that is a plain “yes”. (The four figures add up to 101. The page does not explain it; rounding would do that.)

So the people counted in the 88% said they trust reviews as much as a recommendation if there are several of them, if they seem genuine, or only for some kinds of business. The sentence that travels keeps the 88 and drops the “if”.

The same chart shows the three earlier years. Adding the three “yes” bars the same way gives 67% in 2011, 72% in 2012 and 79% in 2013, the last of which the page also states in words.

The same survey in 2026: 49%

BrightLocal still runs the survey. The 2026 edition carries a publication date of 11 February 2026 in the page’s own data, and a last change on 21 October 2026.4 It says the company has tracked this question for “over 15 years” and that the figure “still sits at a significant 49% today”.

The chart in that edition gives people a statement, “I trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations from friends or family”, and three ways to answer:

  • 48.6%agree
  • 26.1%disagree
  • 25.3%neither

The 2026 survey was answered by 1,002 adults in the United States, through SurveyMonkey. BrightLocal calls the panel representative.

The two numbers should not be read as a fall of 39 points. In 2014 people chose among three kinds of “yes” and one “no”. In 2026 they could agree, disagree or pick neither, and one in four picked neither. The people, the country mix and the way of recruiting them changed as well. I did not read the editions in between, so I do not know when the wording changed.

What these surveys do not show

  • They record what people say about their own trust. Nobody’s choice of a business was observed.
  • The 2014 figure is about local businesses in North America, asked of one company’s own panel. It is not about products, and not about “consumers” in general.
  • Both surveys were run and published by a company that sells software for local search marketing. They are not peer reviewed.
  • Neither one says anything about reviews shown on a business’s own website, or about sales.

What I take from this

  • If you quote the figure, quote the year. 88% is the 2014 answer. The current figure from the same source is 49%, with a quarter of people undecided.
  • The conditions are the useful part. In 2014 the two most chosen answers were about having several reviews to read and believing they are real. In the 2026 edition, the factor people picked most often when judging a review was that it is “backed up by other reviews with similar sentiment”, at 56%. My reading: what earns the trust is a consistent set of reviews, not one glowing one.
  • A figure in a journal is not always a finding of that journal. The 2020 paper passes on the 88% from an infographic. It is worth following a number back one more step.
  • What has been measured with more care is narrower. There are controlled studies on how ratings change which business people choose. I went through them in the article on reviews and choice.

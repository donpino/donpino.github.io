The “50 milliseconds” study measured how a page looks, not whether people stay

November 2026 · Luca

“Visitors form an opinion about your website in 50 milliseconds.” I went looking for the study. It exists, it is peer reviewed, and its own title says much the same. What it measured is narrower than the way it gets quoted: whether a screenshot looks appealing, not whether a visitor trusts the site, stays on it or buys.

The study: three experiments at one university

The paper was published in 2006 in Behaviour & Information Technology by four researchers at Carleton University in Ottawa.1 The participants were university students. Screenshots of home pages flashed on a monitor, one at a time, and after each one the student rated how visually appealing it was. Everyone saw every page twice, in a new random order, to check whether the same person gives the same answer the second time.

  • 22students in the first study: 100 home pages, each shown for 500 milliseconds
  • 31students in the second: the 50 most extreme of those pages, 500 milliseconds each
  • 40students in the third: 20 saw the 50 pages for 50 milliseconds, 20 for 500

So the famous number rests on the third experiment, and on the 20 people in its faster group.

What happened at 50 milliseconds

The result behind the title: the average rating each page got from the 50 millisecond group lined up closely with the average it got from the 500 millisecond group. On the first viewing the correlation was .947, where 1 would be a perfect match. From this the authors conclude that “visual appeal can be assessed within 50 ms”.

The same section of the paper reports the other side of that result.

  • .947correlation between the two groups’ average ratings, first viewing
  • .34 vs .56average agreement between one participant and another, at 50 and at 500 milliseconds
  • 41% vs 2.8%of comparisons between two participants with no significant agreement, at 50 and at 500 milliseconds

As a group, people who saw a page for a twentieth of a second ranked the pages much like people who had ten times longer. One by one they were far less consistent. They agreed less with each other, and less with themselves: at 50 milliseconds, 8 of the 20 had a correlation below .60 between their own first and second rating of the same pages. At 500 milliseconds, none of the 20 did.

Three things that got lost in the retelling

  • It measured visual appeal and nothing else. Nobody was asked whether they trusted the site or would use it. Nobody could stay, leave, click or buy, because the pages were screenshots.
  • The pages were picked to be extreme. Members of the lab collected pages they thought looked very good or very bad. After the first study the middle half was dropped, so the 50 millisecond test used only the 25 best rated and the 25 worst rated. Telling the best from the worst at a glance is an easier task than judging an ordinary page, and the authors say they were not interested in the pages in between.
  • 50 milliseconds is not a threshold. Only two durations were tested, 50 and 500. The authors write that finding an accurate threshold for first impressions was not their aim.

What this study does not show

  • That a first impression changes what people do. No behaviour was measured. The introduction argues, from other research, that a first impression colours later judgments. These three experiments did not test that.
  • How ordinary visitors react. The participants were university students, and the 50 millisecond condition had 20 of them.
  • Anything about phones. The screenshots were taken in Internet Explorer 6 at 1024 by 768 pixels and shown on a 19 inch monitor.
  • What makes a page look good. In the second study, appeal went together with ratings of colour, layout and being well designed, and not with whether a page was simple or complex. When two experts were asked to rate the pages on 89 graphical properties, they agreed with each other on only nine.

What I take from this

  • People do judge the look of a page almost at once. As a group they do it consistently, even after a glimpse. That part of the claim holds.
  • “You have 50 milliseconds” is the title of a paper, not a deadline. It comes from 20 students and 50 pages chosen to be the best and the worst. I would not quote it as a law about every visitor.
  • The look is not the whole first impression. In a different study, design was the reason people gave for leaving a site, and content was the reason they gave for staying and trusting it.
  • What a visitor meets in the first screen goes into an audit, with the page where it was seen. A score for beauty does not. In this study even experts could not agree on what makes a page appealing.

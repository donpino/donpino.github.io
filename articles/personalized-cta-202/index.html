“Personalized CTAs convert 202% better”: the same page said 42% until 2018

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Personalized calls to action convert 202% better than generic ones.” I went looking for where that comes from. It is HubSpot, a company that sells marketing software, writing about its own customers’ data. And at the same web address, for almost five years before, the post said 42%.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “personalized CTAs convert 202% better”. The AI summary said they convert “202% better than generic, one-size-fits-all versions”, based on “an analysis of over 330,000 calls-to-action”.1 The results below it were LinkedIn posts, agency blogs and a higher education marketing firm. I opened that search once.

The page today

The address the figure leads to is on HubSpot’s blog. Today it holds a list called “15 Call-to-Action Statistics You Need to Know About”, updated on 26 September 2025.2 Item three: “After analyzing and comparing more than 330,000 CTAs over a six-month timeframe, we discovered that personalized CTAs convert 202% better than basic CTAs.” To see where that came from, I read the earlier versions of the same address saved by the Internet Archive.

2013: 42%

The first version I found, saved in October 2013, is titled “Personalized Calls-to-Action Convert 42% Better [New Data]”, by Anum Hussain, dated 8 October 2013.3 It says a colleague analysed “over 93,000 calls-to-action from our database of customers”, and that calls to action targeted to the visitor “had a 42% higher view-to-submission rate” than ones that were the same for everybody. The post calls the targeted kind “Smart CTAs”, HubSpot’s name for the feature.

The copies saved in 2014, 2015 and as late as 6 June 2018 still carry the 42% title.

2018: 202%

The copies from September 2018 onwards carry a new title: “Personalized Calls to Action Perform 202% Better Than Basic CTAs [New Data]”, by Jeffrey Vocell, originally published on 28 June 2018.4 I read the copy saved in January 2019. What it says:

  • He “analyzed more than 330,000 CTAs” over six months.
  • He sorted them into three kinds: basic ones, the same for everyone; ones being tested against each other; and “Smart” ones tailored to the visitor, for instance by location, language, or whether they are already a customer.
  • “Personalized CTAs convert 202% better than default versions.” The figures are in charts, which I could not see in the saved copy.

The text does not say what “convert” means here: a click, a form sent, or something else. The 2013 version measured views to submissions; the 2018 one does not say. It gives no conversion rates in the text, no number of visitors, and no test of chance.

The two versions are separate analyses, five years apart, of different sets of calls to action. They do not contradict each other as such. But the address, the subject and the “[New Data]” label stayed the same, and anyone who cited the page before mid 2018 was citing 42%.

What neither version shows

  • Neither was an experiment. They compare calls to action that different customers chose to set up. Businesses that bother to tailor their calls to action may differ in many other ways from those that do not.
  • In both posts’ examples, the tailoring depends on whether the visitor is already a lead or a customer. People who already know the business may respond more whatever the button says.
  • Neither post gives the underlying rates, the number of people, or how the comparison was made.
  • HubSpot sells the feature being measured. The posts are company blog posts, not peer reviewed.

What I take from this

  • Do not quote 202% as a finding. It is a vendor’s summary of its own customers’ data, with no rates published. “HubSpot reported in 2018 that tailored calls to action converted 202% better in its customers’ data” is as far as it goes.
  • A cited page can change under the citation. When you quote a figure from a web page, note the date you read it. This one changed its headline number and author without changing its address.
  • The idea underneath is reasonable. A call to action that fits where the reader is, and says what happens next, makes sense. On a small business site that usually means one plain button per page that matches the page: “Book a consultation” on the service page, not “Submit” or “Learn more”. That is a judgement, not something these posts measured.

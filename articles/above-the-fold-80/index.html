“80% of attention is above the fold” is from 2010. The same company measured 57% in 2018

November 2026 · Luca Panetto

“Users spend 80% of their time above the fold.” I went looking for the study. It is an eye tracking analysis published in 2010 by the Nielsen Norman Group, with 21 people on screens of 1024 by 768 pixels. The same company repeated it in 2018 with 120 people on bigger screens and found 57%. The figure people quote is the older one.

Where I met the number

On 18 November 2026 I searched Google for “users spend 80% of their time above the fold”. The first result was the 2010 study and the second its 2018 update. Further down, a screenshot tool, a booking software blog and several design agencies repeated the 80%, some without a year.1 I opened that search once. No AI summary had finished loading when I read the page.

The 2010 study: 21 people, small screens

“Scrolling and Attention” is by Jakob Nielsen, dated 21 March 2010.2 “Above the fold”, in his words, simply means “viewable without further action”: what is on screen before you scroll. What he measured:

  • Part of a larger eye tracking study the company ran for its own training seminars.
  • He kept only the tasks where people could go to any site they wanted: 21 people, 541 pages, 57,453 fixations, a fixation being a moment when the eye rests on something.
  • The screens were 1024 by 768 pixels.
  • He counted where on the page the viewing time fell.
  • 80.3%of viewing time above the fold
  • 19.7%below it

He added that bigger screens would “somewhat increase” the share above the fold, because more would fit in the first view.

The 2018 repeat: 57%

Eight years later the company published a new analysis under the same title, by Therese Fessenden, dated 15 April 2018.3 More than 130,000 fixations from 120 people, on screens of 1920 by 1080, across news, shopping, blogs, FAQ pages and encyclopedia pages.

  • 57%of viewing time above the fold
  • 17%in the second screenful
  • 74%in the first two screenfuls together
  • 81%in the first three

So the prediction in 2010 went the wrong way: screens got bigger and the share above the fold went down. The 2018 article suggests why: pages had become longer and more spaced out, and people more used to scrolling. It also says it cannot separate the effect of bigger screens from that of new design habits.

What did not change is the shape. In both studies attention falls sharply after the first screen and keeps falling. In 2018, on ordinary websites, more than 42% of the viewing time fell in the top fifth of the page.

What these studies do not show

  • Both were on computer screens. Neither one says anything about phones.
  • They measure where people looked, not what they did. No purchase, booking or enquiry was counted.
  • The 2010 figure rests on 21 people. Both studies were run and published by a consultancy that sells training on this subject, and neither was peer reviewed.
  • The pages were many different kinds, averaged together. Your page may hold attention better or worse.

What I take from this

  • If you quote a figure, quote 57% and the year. 80% is from 2010 and from 1024 by 768 screens. The same company replaced it.
  • The fold is a slope, not a wall. People do scroll; they just give each lower part less time. Both studies agree on that, and it is the part worth keeping.
  • Put the thing that decides the visit first. What the business does, where, and the button to book or buy. The 2018 article says the same: keep the main call to action above the fold.
  • Check your own page on a phone. Neither study was on one, and a phone shows much less in its first screen. Open your page on a phone and see whether what the business does and the button to book or buy are visible before you scroll.

What a website conversion audit is, with a real one to read

Last updated: 13 November 2026

A website conversion audit, also called a CRO audit, is a review of a website that looks for the places where visitors who came to book, buy or get in touch give up, and says what to change. This page explains what one checks, what you should hold in your hands at the end, and links to a full example you can read without leaving an email address.

What it is, and what it is not

CRO stands for conversion rate optimization. “CRO audit”, “conversion audit” and “website conversion audit” are three names for the same piece of work.

“Conversion” is the moment a visitor does the thing the site exists for: books, buys, calls, sends the form. A conversion audit follows the way to that moment and writes down everything that gets in the way.

  • It is not an SEO audit. An SEO audit asks whether people can find the site. A conversion audit starts where that ends: someone has arrived, so what stops them? The kinds of audit are compared in website audit for a small business.
  • It is not an A/B test. A test shows two versions of a page to live visitors and needs enough of them to tell the versions apart. An audit is one careful pass through the site as it is today.
  • It is not a redesign. Most of what an audit finds is fixed by changing a link, a sentence or the place of a button.

What gets checked: the checklist

These are the things I go through on every site. The full list, as questions you can answer on your own site, is the conversion audit checklist.

  • The way to a booking or a purchase. Every button and link that says book, buy or contact, followed to where it ends. Does it land on a page of the same business? I wrote about where booking links lead.
  • Prices. Whether a visitor can find what a thing costs, and how many steps it takes. What the evidence says about hiding prices is less simple than the usual advice.
  • What customers say. Reviews and testimonials on the site, and the business’s reviews on Google. See reviews and choice.
  • Links, buttons, phone numbers and email addresses that do not do what they say. They are more common than you would think.
  • Leftovers. Expired offers, old dates, template text that was never replaced, spelling mistakes.
  • The wording, quoted exactly, where a sentence leaves a visitor unsure what is offered. See wording and decisions.
  • What the site hands to search engines and AI assistants: the page title, the description and the business record in the page code.
  • Google’s AI Mode, asked three times for what the business sells, in its town. I wrote about what AI Mode does to clicks.
  • Google’s public ad library: whether the business is advertising, and what a visitor arriving from an ad meets. That page has its own list: the landing page audit.

What you should get at the end: the template

An audit is only useful if someone can act on it. Mine is a PDF of five to seven pages with this shape:

  1. What is already working. Three things the site gets right. An audit that only lists problems is a sales pitch.
  2. Two to four leaks, one to a page. Each with the same four parts: what I found, why it matters, what fixing it changes, and the fix, down to the words to change.
  3. An order to do them in, cheapest fix first, with the effort each one takes.
  4. What the audit does not contain. No percentage, no revenue estimate, no borrowed industry figure.

A real example, in full

The sample audit is published on this site, as a page and as a PDF. Seven pages, four leaks, three strengths. Every finding in it was seen on a real website and checked in October 2026. The findings come from four different businesses, put together in one report so that none of them can be recognised. The four leaks:

  • Every booking button opens a sign in screen.
  • The price is four steps away from the page that describes the service.
  • The homepage carries no customer’s words, and the reviews page is one small link in the footer.
  • Asked three times, Google’s AI Mode never named the business.

What a conversion audit cannot tell you

  • What your conversion rate “should” be. The figure usually quoted as the average, 2.35%, is a 2014 median of ad accounts. I found no study that measures one across ordinary business websites.
  • How much a fix will earn. An audit shows what a visitor meets. Only your own numbers, before and after, show what changing it did.
  • Anything behind a login. An audit of the public site sees what a new visitor sees, and nothing else.

Doing one yourself

You can do a first pass on your own site in an hour.

  1. Open the site on a phone, in a private window, as someone who has never seen it.
  2. Try to do the one thing the site exists for: book, buy, or get a price.
  3. Each time you hesitate, write down the page and what you saw. Do not fix anything yet.
  4. Press every button and every link in the menu and the footer. Note the ones that lead nowhere or somewhere unexpected.
  5. Ask someone who does not know the business to do the same, and compare the two lists.

The limit of doing it yourself is that you already know where everything is. That is the one thing a new visitor does not.

Homepage audit: 15 checks for the home page of a small business

Last updated: 14 November 2026

A homepage audit looks at one page: the home page. It asks whether a first time visitor can tell what the business does, where the page sends them next, what proof it shows, and what it hands to Google. Below are 15 checks you can run on your own home page, what I found on two real ones, and what the research usually quoted about home pages measured.

What a homepage audit covers

One page, read from the first screen to the footer, and every place it sends a visitor with one press. It is narrower than a conversion audit of a whole site, which follows the visitor on to the service pages, the prices and the booking. It is also a different page from the one an advertisement leads to, which has its own shorter audit.

The name is used loosely. I searched Google for “homepage audit” twice on 14 November 2026, from the United States, in English. Both times the first page held the same nine results, and all nine titles were about auditing a whole website or checking its SEO. One of them also mentioned the home page. If that is what you were looking for, the four kinds of website audit, and the free official tool for each, are on this page.

The audit: 15 checks

Do them on a phone, in a private window, as someone who has never heard of the business. Pages load in stages and pop ups arrive late, so wait a few seconds and look again before writing anything down. Write the date next to each answer.

The first screen

  1. Without scrolling, does the page say what the business does, and where, in plain words? Nielsen Norman Group’s guidelines for home pages start here: one sentence that sums up what the site or the company does, above all for a business that is not famous.1
  2. Is there a button for the main thing a visitor comes to do, such as book, buy or ask, and can it be seen and pressed without scrolling?
  3. Wait ten seconds. Does a pop up, a banner or a chat window cover that button?
  4. Can every line on the first screen be read against what is behind it, including text placed over a photograph or a video?

Where the page sends people

  1. List every button and link on the home page that says book, buy, order or contact. Press each one. Do they all land in the same place, and is it the place their words promise?
  2. Count the starting points the page offers. The same guidelines advise clear starting points for the main one to four things people come to do.1 How many does yours show before the first scroll?
  3. Do buttons that lead to different places carry different words, and do the words say where they lead, such as “See prices”, and not only “Learn more”?
  4. Does every item in the menu and in the footer open a page that exists?

Proof, price and who you are

  1. Does the home page show anything a customer said: a quote, a rating, a review? If those sit on a separate page, is that page in the top menu, or only in the footer?
  2. For the main thing you sell: is a price, or a starting price, on the home page or one press away from it?
  3. Does the page say who is behind the business: a name, a town or an address, a way to reach a person? On a phone, press the phone number and see that it starts a call.
  4. Is there an offer, an event or a date on the home page that has already passed, or text left from the template the site was built on?

What the home page hands to Google

  1. Read the page title, the text shown in the browser tab. Does it carry the name of the business and what it sells, and where? Google says a title should be descriptive and concise, and that it may show a different one if the title does not reflect the page.3
  2. Search Google for the name of the business. Next to the result Google shows a site name. Is it the name of the business, or the bare web address? Google builds that name automatically, and says it draws on the home page, meaning a small record in the page code called WebSite, the title, the headings and other text on the page, and on how the site is referred to around the web.2
  3. Is the name written the same way everywhere on the home page: in the logo, in the title, in the footer? Google asks for the site name to be used consistently across the home page, and says a generic name, such as a description of the trade and the town, is unlikely to be chosen.2

For a whole site, the longer list is the conversion audit checklist. For the way from the Book button to a time you can pick, there is a booking page audit.

What I found on two real home pages

Both belong to small businesses that take bookings, in this case medical spas. Both findings are in the published sample audit, with the measurements and the dates: 2 and 14 October 2026.4

  • Four Book buttons, one sign in screen. The Book button in the menu and three more on the home page all led to the sign in form of the booking system, which asks for a username, an email or a mobile phone. The way on to the actual booking page was a small link lower down on that screen.
  • No customer’s words anywhere on the page. From the first screen to the footer the home page showed no quote, no star rating and no review card. The business had collected testimonials, on a separate reviews page. The only way there from the home page was one link in the footer list, 15 pixels tall, 3,794 pixels down a page 3,982 pixels long. The top menu had no reviews item.
  • And one thing that worked. That same home page showed an award badge dated 2025 at the bottom of its first screen. It was the only mark of trust on the page, and it was in the right place.

Neither leak needed anything new to be built. The booking page existed and so did the testimonials.

What has been measured

Three sources are quoted again and again about home pages. None of them measures what a change to a home page does to the bookings or the sales of a small business.

  • “Visitors judge a site in 50 milliseconds.” The study is real, from 2006, and it did use home pages: university students rated screenshots of them for visual appeal. The famous figure rests on 20 students who saw each page for 50 milliseconds. As a group they ranked the pages much like the students who had ten times longer. It measured how a page looks, not whether anyone trusts it, stays or buys. The study is described here.
  • “94% of first impressions are about design.” The number comes from a 2004 study of fifteen women reading health websites. 94% of their comments about rejecting a site were about its design. In the same study, 83% of the comments about the sites they trusted were about content. It was about health websites in general, not about home pages. The study is described here.
  • The home page guidelines. The ten guidelines from Nielsen Norman Group that two of the checks above lean on were published in 2002.1 They are the advice of a company that tests websites with users and sells courses and reports. The page gives the guidelines, not the figures behind them, and it was written in 2002, before smartphones. I use them for what they are: a careful list of what to look at, not a measurement.

What a homepage audit cannot tell you

  • How many customers a problem costs. Only your own figures, before and after a change, show that.
  • Whether the home page is where your visitors arrive. People also land on other pages, from a search or from an advertisement. Your own analytics show which page they enter on.
  • How fast the page loads. For that, Google’s PageSpeed Insights measures your own page.
  • What Google will show. The title and the site name are chosen by Google’s systems. The checks above cover what the home page gives them to choose from.

Website audit for a small business: which kind you need, and what you can check for free

Last updated: 13 November 2026

“Website audit” is one name for four different jobs. Most of the guides for small businesses that I found on Google mean only one of them, the SEO audit. This page sets the four side by side, names the free official tool for each, repeats what Google itself tells small businesses about paying for an audit, and lists what I find when I open small business websites.

Four things called a website audit

  • An SEO audit asks whether search engines can find the site and understand it.
  • A speed audit asks how long the pages take to load and respond, mostly on a phone.
  • An accessibility audit asks whether people with disabilities can use the site, for example with a keyboard or a screen reader.
  • A conversion audit asks whether a visitor who has arrived can do what they came for: book, buy, call, get a price.

They answer different questions, so a good result in one says nothing about the others. A site can be first on Google and still send every visitor to a booking button that does not work.

What you can check yourself, for free

Google’s own advice is the place to start: “If you run a small local business, you can probably do much of the work yourself.”1 For three of the four audits there is a free tool from the organisation that sets the rules. For the fourth there is no tool, only a method.

Search: Google Search Console

Google describes it as “a free service offered by Google that helps you monitor, maintain, and troubleshoot your site’s presence in Google Search results”.2 It confirms that Google can find and crawl the site, and it shows how often the site appears in Google Search, for which searches, and how often people click. You do not need it to appear in Google, and appearing costs nothing: Google says it “never accepts money to include or rank sites” in its search results.1

Speed: PageSpeed Insights

Google’s PageSpeed Insights “reports on the user experience of a page on both mobile and desktop devices, and provides suggestions on how that page may be improved”.3 It gives two kinds of data. Lab data comes from a test run in a controlled environment. Field data comes from real visitors using Chrome over the previous 28 days. Google’s own note on the difference: lab data “may not capture real-world bottlenecks”. Type in the address of your page, read the phone result first, and write the figures down with the date.

Accessibility: the W3C Easy Checks

The W3C, the body that publishes the web accessibility guidelines, has a page of first checks that need no expertise: the page title, text alternatives for images, headings, colour contrast, text resizing, keyboard access, forms and their labels, moving content, video and audio alternatives.4 The W3C states their limit itself: “A web page could seem to pass these checks, yet still have significant accessibility barriers.”

If your business is in the United States, this is more than good manners. The Department of Justice wrote in its 2022 guidance that it “has consistently taken the position that the ADA’s requirements apply to all the goods, services, privileges, or activities offered by public accommodations, including those offered on the web”.5 “Public accommodations” is the law’s term for businesses open to the public, such as shops, restaurants, hotels and medical offices. The same guidance says the Department has no regulation setting out detailed technical standards for businesses, and that the guidance itself is not legally binding. I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice: read the page, it is short.

Conversion: no tool, a method

No tool can tell you whether a new customer manages to book, because that takes someone trying to book. The method is to go through your own site as a stranger would, on a phone, and write down every place you hesitate. The conversion audit checklist turns that into 33 questions, and this page explains what a conversion audit is.

What a free audit tool cannot tell you

Search for a website audit and some of the results are a box to type your address into, with a score a few seconds later. The ones I saw on the first page of Google on 13 November 2026 were SEO checkers. Two things to keep in mind when you read such a score.

  • The score is the tool’s, not Google’s. Google’s words: “Google doesn’t evaluate or endorse third-party SEO tools, and these tools don’t have access to Google’s internal ranking data.”1 It also advises that, before making significant changes based on a third-party tool’s audit, you check its recommendations against Google’s official guidance.
  • A scan reads the page. It does not use it. The findings in the section below were all made the same way: by opening the page and pressing the button. I have not run those sites through the free checkers, so I cannot tell you what score they would have received. I can tell you that none of the seven is about a title tag or a loading time.

Before you pay someone

Google publishes advice for site owners who are thinking of hiring help with search.1 Three points from it apply to anyone who offers you an audit:

  • What an audit should give you. “An SEO audit should be about giving you realistic estimates of improvement, and an estimate of the work involved.”
  • Guarantees. “No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.” If someone guarantees first place, Google’s advice is to “find someone else”.
  • Access. For an audit, grant read access to Search Console only, not write access.

To these I add three questions of my own, which I think are fair to ask of any audit, mine included:

  • Can you check each finding yourself, by opening the page it names?
  • Does each finding carry the date it was seen? Websites change.
  • Does the report tell you what it could not see or does not know?

What I find on small business websites

These are seven things I found on real websites of small businesses that take bookings, each one seen on the live page. Four of them are in the published sample audit, with the dates they were checked.

  • Every booking button opened a sign in screen, for an account a new customer does not have.
  • The price existed, but four steps away from the page that described the service. That page had no button to book.
  • The home page carried nothing a customer had said. The reviews page was one small link in the footer.
  • Asked three times for what the business sells, in its town, Google’s AI Mode never named it.
  • A booking page still listed the sample services of the template it was built on.
  • Four service buttons all carried the same two words.
  • A button that said “Book your appointment” led to a contact form with no calendar.

None of these needed a redesign or a developer. Each is a link, a sentence or a setting. That is the usual shape of the problem on a small site: the owner built it once, knows where everything is, and has not gone through it as a stranger since. More on this in why a website does not convert.

Which one to do first

This part is my opinion, not a finding. Start from where you lose people, and Search Console can tell you which side that is on.

  • Few people see the site in Google at all: the search side comes first. Google’s SEO Starter Guide is free and written for site owners.
  • People arrive and nothing happens: more visitors will not fix that. Go through the site as a customer first.
  • You run ads: check the page the ad leads to before anything else, with the landing page audit. You are paying for every visit to it.

What no website audit can tell you

  • How much a fix will earn. Only your own figures, before and after the change, show that.
  • What your numbers “should” be. Averages quoted for small business websites describe other people’s sites, and often not what they seem to.
  • That the site is legally compliant. An accessibility check finds barriers. Whether a site meets the law is a question for a lawyer.

Conversion audit cost: what five published price lists say

Last updated: 14 November 2026

A one time website conversion audit costs anywhere from nothing to $25,000, going by the prices that sellers publish. I found no independent survey of conversion audit pricing, so this page does the next best thing: it lists what five companies on the first page of Google say an audit costs, says who each one is, and shows what changes between a $150 audit and a $10,000 one.

The published prices

Every figure below is a seller’s own statement, read on its page on 14 November 2026. Two of the five give their own price. Three give ranges for the market, and none of those three says how many price lists it looked at.

  • $150 to $250, with a four working day turnaround. The price of one independent auditor’s conversion audit, on that person’s own service page.1
  • $3,500 to $10,000. The price of one agency’s audit, sold as a project, on its pricing page.2
  • Free, $0 to $100, $500 to $2,000, $2,000 to $5,000 and up. Four tiers in one company’s guide: doing it yourself, an automated tool, a freelancer, an agency.3
  • $400 to $800, $2,000 to $5,000, $5,000 to $25,000. Three tiers in another guide: a basic quick review, a typical agency audit, a deep agency audit. Its author says the ranges come from agency pricing pages and cost guides.4
  • $97 to $6,000. The two ends one more guide uses as its example: an automated scan and an agency engagement. Its summary puts the top of the range at $10,000.5

Put side by side, the guides agree on the shape more than on the numbers. Two of the three put an automated report at about $100 or less. All three put an agency audit at $2,000 or more.

What moves the price

These are the differences I can see between the offers I read. They are about what is done, not about how good it is.

  • A tool or a person. In two of the three guides the cheapest paid tier is a report generated by software. Above it, someone opens the site and goes through it.
  • One person or a team. The independent auditor delivers a recorded walkthrough and a ranked list of fixes.1 The agency sells its audit as a project next to monthly programs that start at $2,500 a month.2
  • Your data or only your pages. Some audits need your analytics. Others work from outside. The independent auditor above writes that analytics access is welcome but never required.1
  • Findings only, or findings and fixes. An audit usually ends with a list. Who makes the changes, and at what price, is a separate question, and worth asking before you buy.

Seven questions to ask before paying

  1. Is there a complete sample I can read first, not a description of one?
  2. Will every finding be something seen on my own site, with the page and the date, or a general recommendation?
  3. Where does each number in the report come from? A figure about “the average site” is not a figure about yours.
  4. Does a person open my site, or does a tool produce the report?
  5. What access do you need: none, analytics, the site itself?
  6. How many pages will the report be, and when do I get it?
  7. If I want the changes made for me, who makes them and what does that cost?

What mine costs

On the day this page was written, $490, paid once. It is one person’s audit done from outside: no access to your analytics or to your site. You get a PDF with three things the site already does well, two to four leaks with what I measured, and the order to fix them in. The whole sample audit is free to read, with no email address asked. What these figures cannot tell you

  • What audits usually cost. Five sellers that publish a price are not the market.
  • Which audit is better. The price follows who does the work and how long it takes. None of the pages I read measures what an audit finds.
  • Whether an audit pays for itself. That depends on what is found on your site and on whether the changes get made. Only your own figures, before and after, show it.
